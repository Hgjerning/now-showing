THE MAGNIFICENT SEVEN: does concentration come back to bite?

Guess before you scroll: Since 2012, did the 10 largest US stocks beat the other 490 over the following year?
A) They lagged, as the lore says
B) About even
C) They beat them
Comment A, B or C. The answer is below.

In 2012 the ten largest US stocks were 21% of the 500 largest. Today they are 39%. At the dot-com peak in 2000 the S&P 500 figure was 27%.

Market lore says this ends badly:
→ the biggest stocks lag the rest
→ when concentration peaks, the average stock catches up

I wrote both claims down as tests before looking, then ran them on every month since 2012.

Neither showed up.

The then-top-10 beat the other 490 by +5.7% over the following year on average, and did so in 66% of start months. Rising concentration predicted nothing about the catch-up (t = 0.7).

Why did the giants win? A standard factor model explains about half of the lead: they were large, growth-tilted stocks in an era that paid for both. And it was no free lunch: the lead fell 28% into 2022.

Of $70 trillion created since 2012, 39% came from just ten stocks.

The difference from 1972 and 2000: then the leaders cost about twice the market's multiple. In 2024 the premium was about a third.

Concentration is a risk to size, not a date to guess.

Answer: C: by +5.7% per year on average, in 66% of start months.

In the film, seven gunfighters defend a village. In the S&P 500, the village has handed the ten biggest 39% of its savings.
